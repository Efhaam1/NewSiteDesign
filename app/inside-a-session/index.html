A session is not a topic. It is a full hour, ready to teach.

Three of the 213 carry no positions — they are tournament-preparation and game-analysis sessions — so those run seven parts rather than eight. Every other session runs all eight.

60 minutes planned. 43 on the clock.

Five of the eight parts carry minutes. Three do not, in every session in the curriculum: guided practice, the puzzles, and the homework.

That is deliberate and it is the part of the hour a coach owns. The puzzle block is where a class either flies or gets stuck, and a written number there would be a lie in one direction or the other. What the curriculum fixes is the order, the material and the grading; what it leaves is how long eleven particular children need on position four.

The console prints both figures on the same line, so nobody has to work out which one they are looking at.

Eight positions, chosen and graded before a coach opens the file

The answers are in the session. They are just not on the screen.

Every position carries its solution, the whole forced line in notation, an explanation of why each reply is forced, and the mistakes students usually make on it. All of it sits behind one button, because a coach who can see the answer asks a worse question. Press the button and the line becomes steppable: the board plays the combination out one move at a time.

The questions come with the session

A discussion is the part of a lesson that most often becomes whatever the coach thought of in the moment. Session 115 carries 6 questions for it, and there are 1,247 of them across the curriculum — so nobody has to invent the discussion on the drive to class.

They are not comprehension checks. Every one of these has more than one defensible answer, which is what makes a room argue.

Session 115 · teaching flow · questions to ask, verbatim

  1. Before you play move one of a three-move combination, what three things do you need to confirm about the final position to be sure it is checkmate?
  2. If your move one is a check, how do you decide which of the opponent’s king moves is the forced reply — and what if the king has two possible squares?
  3. What is the difference between a forcing move and a strong move, and why does that difference matter when you are calculating a combination?
  4. If you calculate a three-move line and find that move two does not force a reply, is the sequence still a forced combination? What should you do instead?
  5. Why do we say you must calculate to the END of the line before committing to move one — can you describe a situation where stopping the calculation one move early would lead you astray?
  6. In a combination where move one involves a sacrifice, why is it essential to know your opponent’s forced reply before you give up the piece?

Then they play

Calculate-Out-Loud Combination Game — 17 minutes, written for this session and not borrowed from a folder of games.

Students work in pairs at a shared board. The coach or a student sets up a puzzle position from the session (starting with P1–P2 Foundation positions). Before either player touches a piece, the solver must state the full three-move line out loud — ’I play this, you must play that, then I play this and it is checkmate’ — so their partner can listen for any gap in the calculation. Partners rotate solver and checker roles after each puzzle.

What counts as success: Both partners can state a complete, gapless three-move forcing line for at least three positions — correctly identifying why each opponent reply is forced — before executing a single move on the board.

213 activities across 213 sessions — one written for every hour, not a game to burn the last twenty minutes.

  1. 01
    Set up the assigned puzzle position. The solver studies the board in silence for up to 90 seconds, identifying checks, captures, and forced replies — no piece is touched during calculation.
  2. 02
    The solver speaks the full line aloud before moving: state move one, state the forced reply (and why it is the only option), state move two, state the forced reply, state move three, and confirm checkmate. Partner listens and flags any moment the line branches or an opponent reply is skipped.
  3. 03
    Only after the partner agrees the line is complete does the solver execute the moves on the board. If the spoken line reaches checkmate as stated, the pair earns the point and the partner takes over as solver for the next position.
  4. 04
    If the spoken line contains a hole — an unaddressed opponent reply or a move that does not actually force the response claimed — the partner identifies the flaw before any pieces are moved, and the solver has one further 60-second think to correct and re-state the line from the start.

What goes home

Homework is set for every one of the 213 sessions, in three forms: something to do online, something to do over a real board, and something to think about. 171 sessions carry a fourth — an optional challenge for the child who finishes early.

Online · on whatever platform the academy already uses

  • Complete five mate-in-three puzzles on Lichess (puzzle storm or puzzle practice, filter by ’Mate in 3’). Before clicking each move, write or say aloud the full three-move line; aim for at least three clean first-attempt solves.
  • Play three short games (10+0 or 15+0) on Lichess or Chess.com. After each game, use the engine analysis to find one moment where a forced combination existed and reconstruct the full line in the move list.

Over the board · with a parent, a sibling, or alone

  • Replay one of today’s P6–P8 Challenge puzzles from memory — set up the start position without looking at the sheet, then calculate and execute the three-move line. Ask a family member or friend to play Black’s moves as you call them out.
  • Set up the queen-sacrifice back-ranker from the guided example and show it to someone at home, explaining every forced reply out loud as if coaching them. This rehearsal of the explanation deepens the calculation habit.

To think about · written down, not just answered

  • Describe in one or two sentences what makes a reply truly ’forced’ — and give one example from today’s puzzles where you had to stop and check that your opponent really had no other option.
  • Why is a sacrifice in a forcing combination different from a mistake? How does calculating the full line to mate before you play change what a sacrifice means?

Optional · for the child who wants more

  • Find a real game (from any source: a tournament database, a famous game book, or a YouTube analysis video) that contains a forced mate-in-three. Write down the position, the full three-move line, and one sentence explaining why Black’s replies are forced at each step — bring it to the next session to share.

15 minutes, at home.Across the curriculum that is 4,243 written minutes of work after class. Today it travels as words — a coach reads the three lines out, writes them up, or sends them — because a printed pack is on the licence schedule and not in the product yet. What is not missing is the work itself: no coach has to invent it, and no week goes home empty.
