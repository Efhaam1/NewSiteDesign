For coaches

The planning is done. The hour is yours.

You trained to teach chess. The evening before a class is not that. This is a curriculum of 213 ready-to-teach sessions that hands you the whole hour — the order, the positions, the questions, the activity and the homework — and leaves you the part you are actually good at.

What Sunday night becomes

Sunday night, now

  1. plan
  2. search
  3. organise
  4. improvise
  5. teach

Four of those five happen before anyone arrives, and the fifth is the only one a parent is paying for. The worst part is not the hours — it is that the fourth step is silent. Nobody sees a coach improvise, so nobody can tell a good hour from a lucky one.

Sunday night, with a curriculum

  1. open
  2. follow
  3. teach
  4. observe
  5. progress

Reading the session replaces writing it. The two new steps are the ones that were never there: watching what this particular group did with it, and knowing without deciding what they do next.

One claim this page will not make.No number of hours saved. Nobody has measured that yet, so putting a figure on it would be an invention, and this site does not print figures it cannot count. What is countable is what is already written, and that is the rest of this page.

Nine decisions you no longer make

Every line here is a count off the curriculum, not a promise about it.

  1. 01What to teach next

    Every session names the sessions it is taught after — four on the median, 11 at the deepest. The next session is the next session.

    No argument about whether the group is ready.

  2. 02Which positions

    1,640 positions, already chosen for their session and already graded: 557 Foundation, 646 Core, 437 Challenge.

    No hunting a puzzle database for something that nearly fits.

  3. 03How the hour runs

    Eight parts in a fixed order, 1,701 of them across the curriculum. Warm-up, introduction, core explanation, discussion, guided practice, puzzles, activity, homework.

    The shape is never in question, so the thinking goes into the teaching.

  4. 04What to ask

    1,247 questions included in the sessions — the ones with more than one defensible answer, which are the ones a room argues about.

    The discussion is not invented on the drive to class.

  5. 05What they will get wrong

    Every session carries what students wrongly believe and the mistakes they actually play. All 213 of them.

    A misconception gets caught the first time instead of the fourth.

  6. 06How to simplify it

    Written for every session: what to strip out when the group is not getting it. 213 of 213.

    A hard session becomes a teachable one without abandoning the plan.

  7. 07How to stretch the strong ones

    Also written for every session — 213 extension notes.

    The fast child gets more chess, not more waiting.

  8. 08What they do next week

    Homework set for all 213 sessions in three forms: online, over a real board, and something to think about. 171 carry a fourth for whoever finishes early.

    The lesson does not stop when the room empties.

  9. 09What the hour was for

    1,005 stated outcomes across the curriculum — what a student can do at the end that they could not do at the start.

    You can tell whether it worked, in the words the session used.

the week

And during the class

A lesson plan on paper is a document you read before the room fills. The console is built for the other forty minutes — three rails that all stay on screen, because a coach cannot scroll while eleven children wait.

It is the same session either way. Prep is the document; Teach is the screen you stand in front of.

Open it and press the keys
  • The answer stays hidden. One key reveals it, so the room gets asked first.
  • The clock is per segment, not per lesson. Eight minutes of warm-up looks like eight minutes.
  • Arrow keys step the forced line. The board plays the combination out one move at a time while you talk.
  • Every position is one paste away. FEN for a board, PGN for the whole line, onto whatever screen the room actually has.
  • The positions are numbered and jumpable. Skip position six, come back to it, or stop at four — the session does not lose its place.
  • Pressing a part of the hour goes to it. A coach running late can see exactly what they are about to cut.

Two groups, one preparation

A Tuesday group of six-year-olds and a Thursday group of eleven-year-olds can be taught the same session. What changes is the shape of the hour, not the hour.

Because every position in the curriculum is already graded, the difference is something a coach executes rather than prepares: hand out five positions or hand out eight, run five-minute blocks or fifteen, start full games in week one or hold them back. The session plan is the same either way.

Explorer · ages 5–7

5–8 minute blocks. Foundation and Core are the set, Challenge is the coach’s to show. Mini-games before full games.

Challenger · ages 8–12

10–15 minute blocks. All eight, with the Challenge tier the point of the hour. Notation and full games early. The compressed Stage 1 map runs its 41 sessions as 24 classes, pairing the teaching sessions and never merging a review or an assessment.

set per group · a coach can override per child

A track is a setting on a group.An academy chooses it, and a coach can override it for one child — age is not level, and a strong seven-year-old runs Challenger. Both tracks reach the same outcomes; the difference is how, not what.

What you still do, and what is not here yet

A coach can tell when a product is being sold past what it does. So here is the line.

What the curriculum will never do

  • Teach the lesson. The curriculum’s own rule is that a lesson which only works in an expert’s hands is a badly designed lesson — but a person still has to run the room.
  • Decide how long position four takes. Three of the eight parts carry no minutes on purpose. That elasticity is yours.
  • Know your students. The notes say what children usually believe. They cannot say what yours believe.

What is not built yet

  • A printed homework pack. The homework is written for every session; a pack you can hand out is on the licence schedule, not in the product.
  • Marking a session taught, or tracking a child's progress. The checkpoints are written content, not a grade book.
  • A parent report. There are no child accounts and no child emails, ever — and no report generator yet either.
