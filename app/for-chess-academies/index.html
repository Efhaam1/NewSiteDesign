For academies

Two good coaches. Two different courses.

This is not a discipline problem and it is not solved by hiring better. Two capable coaches given the same group and the same hour will teach two different courses, because nothing written down says which course it is. A curriculum is that document.

    WrittenEvery session names its prerequisites — four on the median, 11 at the deepest, and not one forward reference in the 213.

  3. 03How much practice

    One coach lectures and one coach drills. Both are defensible; they are not the same course.

  4. 04What goes home

    WrittenSet for all 213 sessions, in three forms, before anyone opens the file.

  5. 05What “done” means

    Without a definition, finishing a topic means the coach felt finished with it.

What “the same lesson” actually means

Precisely, and countably. Two coaches teaching session 115 in the same week are teaching the same eight parts, the same eight graded positions, the same six questions and the same stated outcomes — in the same place in the same order.

The assessment is content, not software.The curriculum contains the assessment: the positions, the pass mark and what to do with a student who does not clear it. What it does not contain is a grade book — nothing marks a session taught or stores a child's score, and there are no child accounts and no child emails, ever. An academy that wants a record keeps it the way it keeps one now.

A coach in their second month

The hardest thing about a new trainer is not chess. It is that they have to invent an hour while they are still learning how a room behaves, and nobody finds out how it went.

A complete session changes what onboarding is. A new coach reads the prep sheet the night before: the objective, what a student should be able to do at the end, the thinking routine, what this session builds on, and then each of the eight parts in full. Then five sections that are not about chess at all.

All five are written in every one of the 213 sessions, which is what lets an academy put a second-month coach in front of a group without hoping.

See the prep sheet→
  • What they wrongly believe. The misconceptions this particular subject produces.
  • What they will play. The mistakes students actually make on these positions.
  • How to teach it. The coaching tips for this hour, not general advice.
  • If they are stuck. What to strip out and still teach the session.
  • If they are ahead. Where to take the child who is already there.

A coach resigns on Friday. Tuesday still runs.

Whoever covers the group opens the same session the group was going to get, reads it, and teaches it. The course does not restart, and it does not fork.

This is the operational case for writing a curriculum down, and it is the one an owner feels first. When the hour lives in one coach's head, that coach is a single point of failure — not because they are unreliable, but because nothing else knows what Tuesday was supposed to be.

When the hour is written, cover is a reading task. The prerequisites say where the group is; the session says what happens; the notes say what will go wrong. A stand-in does not have to be as good as the coach they are covering. They have to be able to read.

What an academy sets, and what a coach can override

One syllabus runs two ways. An academy picks the track for a group; a coach can override it for one child, because age is not level and a strong seven-year-old runs the harder one.

This is not two curriculums to maintain, and it is not a lesson that rewrites itself. Every position in the curriculum is already graded, so the difference between the two tracks is which of the eight are the set for everyone, how long a block runs, and how soon a full game replaces a mini-game. Both reach the same outcomes.

Explorer · ages 5–7

5–8 minute blocks. Foundation and Core are the set, Challenge is the coach’s to show. Mini-games before full games.

Challenger · ages 8–12

10–15 minute blocks. All eight, with the Challenge tier the point of the hour. Notation and full games early. The compressed Stage 1 map runs its 41 sessions as 24 classes, pairing the teaching sessions and never merging a review or an assessment.
