The curriculum

Every session, in the order it is taught.

The Efhaam chess curriculum: five stages, ten levels, 30 units, 213 ready-to-teach sessions — from naming the squares in the first one to sitting a graduation paper in the last. Every session lists the sessions it is taught after, so number 213 stands on all 212 in front of it.

  • 5stages
  • 10levels
  • 30units
  • 213sessions, ready to teach
  • 1,640positions, already graded
  • 211hours of class

Four words hold the whole shape.

Stage

5 of them, named for a piece

A stage is one big subject and roughly two years of a child’s chess. Pawn is the rules and the first tactics; Queen is analysis and style. Each declares the age a child usually enters it and the strength that child is usually playing at.

Level

10, two inside every stage

A level is 20–22 sessions, and the last session of every one of the 10 is a graded one: five are called checkpoints, five are stage graduations. Nothing else ends a level.

Unit

30, three inside every level

A unit is one idea taught across 6 to 8 sessions — the fork family, pawn structure, rook endgames. Every unit has at least one review session inside it, and there are 34 of those across the curriculum.

Session

213, and one is one class

One session is one lesson: 50 to 75 minutes, most of them 60. Eight parts in a fixed order, up to eight graded puzzle positions, one activity, and homework in three forms. See what one hour contains.

Nothing here is optional.Only the first session stands on nothing. Every other one names the sessions it comes after — four of them on the median, 11 at the deepest — and there is not a forward reference or a dangling one anywhere in the 213. That is what a curriculum is for: a coach never has to decide what a group is ready for, and a child never meets an idea before the idea it rests on.

The same sessions, two ways to run them.

Age is not level. A group of six-year-olds and a group of eleven-year-olds are taught the same session; what changes is how long a block runs, which of the eight positions are the set for everyone, and how soon a full game replaces a mini-game.

A track is a setting, not a second syllabus.Both tracks reach the same outcomes — the difference is how, not what. Every position in the curriculum is already graded Foundation, Core or Challenge — 557, 646 and 437 of the 1,640 puzzle positions — and that grading is what lets one coach hand out five positions and another hand out eight from the same page.

Explorer · ages 5–7

5–8 minute blocks. Foundation and Core are the set, Challenge is the coach’s to show. Mini-games before full games.

Challenger · ages 8–12

10–15 minute blocks. All eight, with the Challenge tier the point of the hour. Notation and full games early. The compressed Stage 1 map runs its 41 sessions as 24 classes, pairing the teaching sessions and never merging a review or an assessment.
